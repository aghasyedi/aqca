Trace Distance

1. What is Trace Distance?

While Fidelity measures how "close" or "overlapping" two quantum states are, the Trace Distance is the ultimate metric for how "distinguishable" they are. It is the quantum generalization of the classical Total Variation Distance between probability distributions.

2. Mathematical Definition

For two density matrices \(\rho\) and \(\sigma\), the trace distance \(D(\rho, \sigma)\) is defined as half of the trace norm of their difference:

\(D(\rho, \sigma) = \frac{1}{2} ||\rho - \sigma||_{\text{tr}} = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} \left| \rho - \sigma \right|\)

Where the absolute value of a matrix \(A\) is defined as \(|A| = \sqrt{A^\dagger A}\). Because \(\rho - \sigma\) is Hermitian, its eigenvalues are real, and the trace norm is simply the sum of the absolute values of its eigenvalues.

Trace distance is bounded between 0 and 1:

  • \(D = 0\) means the states are identical (indistinguishable).
  • \(D = 1\) means the states are perfectly orthogonal (perfectly distinguishable).

3. The Operational Interpretation

The trace distance is not just a mathematical abstraction; it has a profound, real-world operational meaning.

The Distinguishability Game: Suppose a referee secretly prepares a quantum state. They flip a fair classical coin. If heads, they prepare state \(\rho\). If tails, they prepare state \(\sigma\). They hand this unknown single qubit to you.

Your task is to perform a single Measurement (using any combination of projectors or POVMs you want) to guess whether you were handed \(\rho\) or \(\sigma\).

No matter how clever your measurement strategy is, your maximum possible probability of guessing correctly is strictly bounded by the trace distance:

\(P_{\text{guess}} \le \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} D(\rho, \sigma)\)

If \(D=1\), you can always guess correctly (\(P=1\)). If \(D=0\), the states are identical, and you are reduced to randomly guessing (\(P = 0.5\)).

4. The Fuchs-van de Graaf Inequalities

Because Fidelity (\(F\)) measures overlap and Trace Distance (\(D\)) measures distinguishability, they are inversely related. This exact relationship was proven by Fuchs and van de Graaf, establishing that if you know one, you can bound the other:

\(1 - \sqrt{F(\rho, \sigma)} \le D(\rho, \sigma) \le \sqrt{1 - F(\rho, \sigma)}\)

For the special case where at least one of the states is pure, the upper and lower bounds converge perfectly, yielding the exact equality:

\(D(|\psi\rangle, \rho) = \sqrt{1 - F(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|, \rho)}\)
