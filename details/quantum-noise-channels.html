Quantum Noise Channels

1. The Open Quantum System

In idealized quantum mechanics, systems are perfectly isolated and evolve according to unitary matrices. In reality, qubits interact with their surrounding environment (heat, electromagnetic fluctuations). Because we cannot track the massive environment, the pure state of our qubit becomes entangled with the environment, resulting in a mixed state.

To mathematically describe mixed states and non-unitary evolution, we must abandon state vectors \(|\psi\rangle\) and use Density Matrices \(\rho\).

2. Kraus Operators (CPTP Maps)

The evolution of an open quantum system is described by a Completely Positive Trace-Preserving (CPTP) map, often called a Quantum Channel \(\mathcal{E}\). It is represented using a set of Kraus operators \(\{E_k\}\):

\(\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_k E_k \rho E_k^\dagger\)

To ensure probability is conserved (the trace of the density matrix remains 1), the operators must satisfy the completeness relation: \(\sum_k E_k^\dagger E_k = I\).

3. Deriving Standard Noise Channels

Let's construct the Kraus operators for the most common sources of error in quantum hardware.

Bit-Flip Channel

With probability \(p\), the environment applies a Pauli-X gate (flipping 0 to 1 and vice versa). With probability \(1-p\), nothing happens. This destroys Fidelity.

\(E_0 = \sqrt{1-p} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_1 = \sqrt{p} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\)

Phase-Flip Channel

Unique to quantum mechanics! The bit value doesn't change, but with probability \(p\), the relative phase is flipped (Pauli-Z). It transforms \(|+\rangle\) into \(|-\rangle\). This destroys superpositions without affecting classical probabilities.

\(E_0 = \sqrt{1-p} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_1 = \sqrt{p} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\)

Depolarizing Channel

This is the "worst-case scenario" channel. With probability \(p\), the qubit is completely randomized (replaced by the maximally mixed state \(I/2\)). It models symmetric error in all three Pauli axes (X, Y, Z).

\(E_0 = \sqrt{1-p} I, \quad E_1 = \sqrt{\frac{p}{3}} X, \quad E_2 = \sqrt{\frac{p}{3}} Y, \quad E_3 = \sqrt{\frac{p}{3}} Z\)

Amplitude Damping Channel

Unlike the others, this channel is highly asymmetric. It models the loss of energy (relaxation). If a qubit is in the excited \(|1\rangle\) state, it has a probability \(\gamma\) of emitting a photon into the environment and decaying to \(|0\rangle\). If it is already in \(|0\rangle\), it cannot decay further.

\(E_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{1-\gamma} \end{bmatrix}, \quad E_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \sqrt{\gamma} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\)

Notice that \(E_1\) is not a unitary matrix! It physically moves amplitude from \(|1\rangle\) into \(|0\rangle\). This channel is the direct physical cause of the \(T_1\) Decoherence Time.
