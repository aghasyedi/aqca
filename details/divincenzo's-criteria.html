DiVincenzo's Criteria

1. Introduction

In 2000, theoretical physicist David P. DiVincenzo published a landmark paper establishing the exact physical requirements needed to construct a functional quantum computer. These criteria remain the gold standard against which all modern quantum hardware (superconducting, trapped ions, photonics, etc.) is evaluated.

2. The Five Criteria for Quantum Computation

  1. A scalable physical system with well characterized qubits:
    The system must act strictly as a two-level system. If you are using energy levels of an atom, you must ensure that microwave pulses transition the atom from \(|0\rangle \rightarrow |1\rangle\) without accidentally exciting it to \(|2\rangle\) or \(|3\rangle\). Furthermore, you must be able to add more qubits (scalability) without the system falling apart due to crosstalk.
  2. The ability to initialize the state of the qubits to a simple fiducial state:
    Computation requires a blank slate. Before a circuit begins, the system must be cooled and reset to the \(|00...0\rangle\) state with extremely high Fidelity.
  3. Long relevant decoherence times, much longer than the gate operation time:
    Quantum information decays due to Quantum Noise. The ratio of the Decoherence Times (\(T_1\), \(T_2\)) to the gate time must be roughly \(10^4\) or \(10^5\) to allow error correction algorithms (see the Threshold Theorem) to fix errors faster than they occur.
  4. A "universal" set of quantum gates:
    The hardware must be able to execute a small set of fundamental gates that can approximate any \(n\)-qubit unitary matrix to arbitrary precision. A standard universal set is arbitrary single-qubit rotations combined with the two-qubit CNOT gate.
  5. A qubit-specific measurement capability:
    We must be able to perform a projective Measurement on a single specific qubit, forcing it into a classical 0 or 1, without destroying the quantum coherence of the other \(n-1\) qubits in the processor.

3. The Two Criteria for Quantum Communication

To network quantum computers together to form a "Quantum Internet" (enabling distributed quantum computing and quantum cryptography), DiVincenzo added two network criteria:

  1. The ability to interconvert stationary and flying qubits:
    A stationary qubit (like a superconducting transmon fixed in a dilution refrigerator) must be able to securely transfer its quantum state to a flying qubit (like a photon) to travel long distances.
  2. The ability to faithfully transmit flying qubits between specified locations:
    The flying qubits must be transmitted via fiber optic cables or free-space satellite links with minimal photon loss and without destroying their delicate entanglement.
