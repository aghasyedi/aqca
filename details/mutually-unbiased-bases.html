Mutually Unbiased Bases

1. Mathematical Definition in Arbitrary Dimensions

In a \(d\)-dimensional Hilbert space, two orthonormal bases \(\mathcal{B}_1 = \{|\psi_1\rangle, \dots, |\psi_d\rangle\}\) and \(\mathcal{B}_2 = \{|\phi_1\rangle, \dots, |\phi_d\rangle\}\) are said to be Mutually Unbiased Bases (MUBs) if the inner product between any state in the first basis and any state in the second basis has a constant magnitude.

Mathematically, for all \(i\) and \(j\) in \(\{1, \dots, d\}\):

\(|\langle \psi_i | \phi_j \rangle|^2 = \frac{1}{d}\)

This means that if a system is prepared in a state belonging to \(\mathcal{B}_1\), and a measurement is performed in the basis \(\mathcal{B}_2\), all \(d\) possible outcomes are equally likely. The measurement result is completely random, yielding maximum Heisenberg Uncertainty.

2. MUBs for a Single Qubit (d = 2)

For a single qubit (\(d=2\)), the maximum number of mutually unbiased bases is \(d+1 = 3\). These three bases correspond perfectly to the eigenstates of the three Pauli matrices (X, Y, and Z):

  • Z-basis (Rectilinear): The computational basis \(\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\). Eigenstates of the Pauli-Z operator.
  • X-basis (Diagonal): \(\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}\), where \(|\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle)\). Eigenstates of the Pauli-X operator.
  • Y-basis (Circular): \(\{|+i\rangle, |-i\rangle\}\), where \(|\pm i\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle \pm i|1\rangle)\). Eigenstates of the Pauli-Y operator.

Let's verify the unbiased condition between the Z-basis and X-basis:

\(|\langle 0 | + \rangle|^2 = \left| \langle 0 | \left( \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \right) \right|^2 = \left| \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\)

3. The BB84 Protocol (Quantum Cryptography)

MUBs are not just mathematical curiosities; they are the physical foundation of quantum cryptography, specifically Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) protocols like BB84 (developed by Bennett and Brassard in 1984).

In BB84, Alice wants to send a secret cryptographic key to Bob. She encodes random bits (0 or 1) using randomly chosen bases (either Z or X). She sends these photons to Bob, who measures them using randomly chosen bases (Z or X).

The Eavesdropper's Dilemma

Suppose an eavesdropper, Eve, intercepts a photon. Because of the No-Cloning Theorem, she cannot copy it. She must measure it to read it. But she doesn't know which basis Alice used!

If Alice sent a \(|+\rangle\) state (X-basis, representing a bit 0), and Eve guesses incorrectly and measures in the Z-basis, Wavefunction Collapse forces the state to become either \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) with 50% probability.

When Bob finally receives the photon and measures it in the correct X-basis, he will no longer get \(|+\rangle\) with 100% certainty; he has a 50% chance of getting \(|-\rangle\). Eve's measurement in an unbiased basis introduced a detectable statistical error rate (ideally 25%) into the transmission, alerting Alice and Bob to the eavesdropping attempt.
