Quantum State Tomography

1. The Problem of State Reconstruction

When you build a quantum circuit, you want to know if the hardware actually generated the state you asked for. But due to Wavefunction Collapse, you cannot look at a single qubit to see its full density matrix.

Quantum State Tomography (QST) is the procedure of reconstructing the complete, unknown density matrix \(\rho\) of a quantum system by performing measurements on a large ensemble of identical copies of that state.

2. Tomography of a Single Qubit

Any 1-qubit density matrix can be completely parameterized by a 3-dimensional vector \(\vec{r} = (r_x, r_y, r_z)\) called the Bloch vector. The length of this vector is \(|\vec{r}| \le 1\).

\(\rho = \frac{1}{2} (I + r_x X + r_y Y + r_z Z)\)

To determine \(\rho\), the experimentalist must determine \(r_x, r_y, r_z\). It turns out these components are exactly the expectation values of the Pauli observables!

  • Prepare 10,000 copies, measure them in the X-basis. The average outcome (number of +1s minus number of -1s divided by total) is \(r_x = \langle X \rangle\).
  • Prepare another 10,000 copies, measure them in the Y-basis. The average is \(r_y = \langle Y \rangle\).
  • Prepare another 10,000 copies, measure them in the Z-basis. The average is \(r_z = \langle Z \rangle\).

Because X, Y, and Z are Mutually Unbiased Bases, measuring one yields no information about the others, which is why we must prepare fresh copies for each basis.

3. The Trouble with Statistics: Maximum Likelihood Estimation

In theory, the linear inversion above works perfectly. In practice, experimental measurements suffer from shot noise (finite statistics) and Quantum Noise.

Because of this noise, if you just plug the raw averages \(r_x, r_y, r_z\) into the formula, the resulting vector length \(|\vec{r}|\) might be greater than 1! This produces a mathematically invalid density matrix with negative probabilities (negative eigenvalues).

To fix this, modern QST uses Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). Instead of directly solving the equation, a computer runs an optimization algorithm to search through all valid, physical density matrices to find the one that most likely produced the observed experimental data.

4. The Scalability Bottleneck

While QST is essential, it does not scale. To fully characterize an \(n\)-qubit state, you must measure the expectation values of \(4^n - 1\) different Pauli operators. For just 10 qubits, that requires over a million different measurement settings, each requiring thousands of circuit executions. This exponential scaling forces researchers to rely on partial techniques (like Shadow Tomography) for larger systems.
