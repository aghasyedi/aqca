Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

1. The Generalized Robertson-Schrödinger Relation

While often introduced in the context of position (\(x\)) and momentum (\(p\)), the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle applies to any two non-commuting Hermitian observables \(A\) and \(B\).

The standard deviation (or uncertainty) of an observable \(A\) is defined as \(\Delta A = \sqrt{\langle A^2 \rangle - \langle A \rangle^2}\). For any quantum state \(|\psi\rangle\), the product of the uncertainties of two observables is strictly bounded by the generalized Robertson-Schrödinger inequality:

\(\Delta A \cdot \Delta B \ge \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{ |\langle [A, B] \rangle|^2 + |\langle \{A, B\} \rangle - 2\langle A \rangle \langle B \rangle|^2 }\)

where \([A, B] = AB - BA\) is the commutator, and \(\{A, B\} = AB + BA\) is the anti-commutator. Because the second term inside the square root is always non-negative, this is often simplified to the more famous Robertson uncertainty relation:

\(\Delta A \cdot \Delta B \ge \frac{1}{2} |\langle [A, B] \rangle|\)

2. Uncertainty in Qubits (Pauli Matrices)

In quantum computing, our primary observables are the Pauli matrices (\(X\), \(Y\), and \(Z\)). These operators do not commute. Let's examine the commutator between \(X\) and \(Z\):

\([X, Z] = XZ - ZX = -iY - (iY) = -2iY\)

Plugging this into the Robertson relation yields:

\(\Delta X \cdot \Delta Z \ge |\langle Y \rangle|\)

A Concrete Example

Suppose our qubit is prepared exactly in the \(|0\rangle\) state. Because \(|0\rangle\) is an eigenstate of the \(Z\) operator, measuring in the Z-basis yields \(+1\) with 100% certainty. The variance \(\Delta Z = 0\).

To satisfy the inequality, what must \(\Delta X\) be? The expectation value \(\langle Y \rangle\) for the state \(|0\rangle\) is 0. However, using the Robertson-Schrödinger relation (with the anti-commutator), we find that because \(X\) and \(Z\) are Mutually Unbiased Bases, preparing a state with zero uncertainty in \(Z\) forces maximum uncertainty in \(X\).

If you measure \(|0\rangle\) in the X-basis, you have a 50% chance of getting \(+1\) (\(|+\rangle\)) and a 50% chance of getting \(-1\) (\(|-\rangle\)). The expectation value \(\langle X \rangle = 0\), and \(\langle X^2 \rangle = 1\), giving \(\Delta X = 1\), the maximum possible uncertainty for a Pauli measurement.

3. Physical Interpretation

It is vital to understand that this uncertainty is not due to clumsy experimental equipment or human ignorance. It is an intrinsic mathematical property of waves (Fourier transforms) and Hilbert space. A quantum state literally cannot possess a well-defined value for both \(X\) and \(Z\) simultaneously.
