Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA)

Flagship & Hybrid Models

1. Overview & Problem Definition

The Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA) is a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm designed to find approximate solutions to NP-hard combinatorial optimisation problems, such as Max-Cut, the Travelling Salesperson Problem, and Boolean Satisfiability (3-SAT).

Rather than attempting to calculate exact solutions deterministically, QAOA encodes the cost function of a classical optimisation problem into a problem Hamiltonian (\(H_C\)). It then uses a parametrised, layered quantum circuit to prepare a quantum state that concentrates probability amplitude around bitstrings that satisfy or optimize the cost function.

Classical Complexity

NP-hard (\(\mathcal{O}(2^N)\) brute force)

Finding exact global optima for worst-case instances scales exponentially with graph size \(N\).

Quantum Speedup

Heuristic / NISQ-friendly

Provides polynomial-to-superpolynomial approximate solutions on near-term, shallow-depth quantum processors.

2. Intuition

QAOA is a discrete, shallow-circuit approximation of the Quantum Adiabatic Theorem.

The Quantum Adiabatic Theorem states that if you start a quantum system in the known, easy-to-prepare ground state of an initial "mixer" Hamiltonian (\(H_M\)) and evolve the system slowly enough toward a target "problem" Hamiltonian (\(H_C\)), the system will remain in its ground state—which at the end corresponds to the optimal classical solution.

Because continuous adiabatic evolution requires circuit depths far too long for modern hardware, QAOA breaks this evolution down into \(p\) discrete alternating layers (known as circuit depth \(p\)):

  1. Apply Problem Phase Shift: Apply the cost Hamiltonian \(H_C\) for a duration \(\gamma_k\). This shifts the quantum phases of candidate solutions based on how well they satisfy the constraints.
  2. Apply Mixer Phase Shift: Apply the mixer Hamiltonian \(H_M\) for a duration \(\beta_k\). This drives quantum interference between candidate solutions, allowing amplitudes to flow toward better configurations.
  3. Classical Optimisation: A classical loop evaluates the average cost of the measured bitstrings and adjusts the parameters \((\vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta})\) to guide the circuit toward higher-quality solutions in subsequent runs.

3. Required Gates & Circuit Schematic

  • Hadamard (\(H^{\otimes n}\)): Prepares the initial uniform superposition \(|+\rangle^{\otimes n}\), which is the ground state of the standard mixer Hamiltonian.
  • Cost Unitary (\(U(H_C, \gamma) = e^{-i \gamma H_C}\)): Implemented using entangling two-qubit \(RZ\) or \(CX\)-\(RZ\)-\(CX\) sub-circuits across graph edges to penalize or reward candidate bitstrings.
  • Mixer Unitary (\(U(H_M, \beta) = e^{-i \beta H_M}\)): Implemented using single-qubit \(RX(2\beta)\) rotations across all qubits.

4. Mathematical Proof & State Evolution

Step 1: Initialisation & Hamiltonians

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) alternates two operators to find approximate solutions to combinatorial problems (like Max-Cut).

First, the objective function is encoded into a diagonal **Cost Hamiltonian** $H_C$. We also define a **Mixer Hamiltonian** $H_M = \sum_i X_i$ to drive transitions between basis states.

The system is initialised in the absolute ground state of the mixer Hamiltonian, which is an equal superposition of all possible $2^n$ candidates:

\[ |\psi_0\rangle = |+\rangle^{\otimes n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^n} |x\rangle \]

Step 2: Alternating Operator Evolution

For a specified depth $p$, QAOA alternates the unitary time-evolution of $H_C$ and $H_M$ using classical variational parameters $\vec{\gamma}$ and $\vec{\beta}$.

• Cost Layer ($e^{-i \gamma_k H_C}$): Applies a relative phase proportional to the classical cost of each state. Good solutions accumulate different phases than poor ones (phase separation).

• Mixer Layer ($e^{-i \beta_k H_M}$): Applies $X$-rotations to redistribute probability amplitude. The phases created by $H_C$ cause constructive interference for good solutions and destructive interference for poor ones.

The complete parameterised trial state after $p$ layers is:

\[ |\vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta}\rangle = \left( \prod_{k=1}^p e^{-i \beta_k H_M} e^{-i \gamma_k H_C} \right) |+\rangle^{\otimes n} \]

Step 3: Expectation Value & Measurement

The objective is to maximize the expected value of the cost function, evaluated as the expectation value of $H_C$:

\[ F_p(\vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta}) = \langle \vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta} | H_C | \vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta} \rangle \]

Because $H_C$ is diagonal in the computational $Z$-basis, we measure the final state multiple times (shots) to yield classical bitstrings $z$. The expectation value is statistically approximated by averaging the classical cost scores $C(z)$ of these measured strings:

\[ F_p(\vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta}) \approx \frac{1}{N_{shots}} \sum_{m=1}^{N_{shots}} C(z_m) \]

Step 4: Classical Optimization & Convergence

The evaluated scalar $F_p(\vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta})$ is passed to a classical numerical optimizer. The optimizer updates the continuous parameters $\vec{\gamma}$ and $\vec{\beta}$ to find the global maximum:

\[ (\vec{\gamma}_{opt}, \vec{\beta}_{opt}) = \text{argmax}_{\vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta}} F_p(\vec{\gamma}, \vec{\beta}) \]

This hybrid loop repeats iteratively. As the optimizer converges, the quantum state is driven towards the highest-energy eigenstate of $H_C$.

Conclusion: Measuring the optimized state yields a bitstring with a high probability of being the optimal (or near-optimal) solution. As the circuit depth $p \to \infty$, QAOA mathematically mimics Adiabatic Quantum Computing, guaranteeing convergence to the exact global optimum.

5. Interactive Visualisation

  • Graph Partitioning View: A dynamic rendering of a graph (e.g., a 4-node ring or complete graph). As parameters update, nodes glow according to their assignment to Set 0 or Set 1, highlighting cut edges in real time.
  • Cost Expectation Curve: A 3D or contour surface map over \((\gamma_1, \beta_1)\) showing local peaks and valleys, tracking the optimizer trajectory toward maximum cut value.

6. Python Code Implementation

Qiskit Program 
import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, QuantumRegister, ClassicalRegister, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector, DensityMatrix, Operator
from qiskit.visualization import plot_histogram, plot_bloch_multivector, plot_state_qsphere, array_to_latex
from IPython.display import display, Markdown

# ==========================================
# CONFIGURATION & PROBLEM DEFINITION
# ==========================================
num_nodes = 3
edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 0)]  # 3-node triangle graph
gamma = 0.3                       # Problem angle
beta = 0.4                        # Mixer angle

# 1. Set up Registers & Circuit
qr = QuantumRegister(num_nodes, name="q")
cr = ClassicalRegister(num_nodes, name="c")
qc = QuantumCircuit(qr, cr)

# 2. Initialisation: Equal Superposition |+>^n
qc.h(qr)
qc.barrier(label="Init")

# 3. Problem Layer (Cost Hamiltonian: U(C, gamma))
for u, v in edges:
    qc.cx(qr[u], qr[v])
    qc.rz(2 * gamma, qr[v])
    qc.cx(qr[u], qr[v])
qc.barrier(label="Problem Layer")

# 4. Mixer Layer (Transverse Field Hamiltonian: U(B, beta))
for node in range(num_nodes):
    qc.rx(2 * beta, qr[node])
qc.barrier(label="Mixer Layer")

# ==========================================
# EXTRACT EXACT MATH BEFORE MEASUREMENT
# ==========================================
unitary = Operator(qc)
final_state = Statevector(qc)
final_density = DensityMatrix(qc)

# 5. Measurement
qc.measure(qr, cr)

# 6. Execute on AerSimulator
simulator = AerSimulator()
tqc = transpile(qc, simulator)
job = simulator.run(tqc, shots=1024)
result = job.result()

# ==========================================
# REQUIRED COURSEWORK OUTPUTS
# ==========================================

display(Markdown(f"QAOA Max-Cut Analysis: {num_nodes}-Node Triangle Graph"))
print(f"Graph Edges: {edges}")
print(f"Parameters: gamma = {gamma}, beta = {beta}")

display(Markdown("1. Circuit Visualisation"))
display(qc.draw('mpl'))

display(Markdown("2. Unitary Evolution"))
if num_nodes <= 3:
    print(f"The {2**num_nodes}x{2**num_nodes} unitary matrix describing the QAOA circuit:")
    display(array_to_latex(unitary.data, prefix="U_{QAOA} = ", max_size=16))
else:
    print(f"Unitary matrix omitted: {num_nodes} qubits results in a matrix too large for clean display.")

display(Markdown("3. Quantum State Representation"))
print("Exact Statevector prior to measurement:")
display(final_state.draw('latex'))

print("\nFinal DensityMatrix prior to measurement:")
display(final_density.draw('latex'))

display(Markdown("4. State Visualisation"))
print("State prior to measurement (Bloch Spheres):")
display(plot_bloch_multivector(final_state))

print("\nState prior to measurement (Q-Sphere):")
display(plot_state_qsphere(final_state))

display(Markdown("5. Measurement Analysis"))
counts = result.get_counts()
print(f"Raw measurement counts: {counts}")
display(plot_histogram(counts))

# 6. Final Max-Cut Verification Logic
display(Markdown("6. Verification: Max-Cut Evaluation"))
print("Evaluating cut values for each measured bitstring (partition cut size):")

def evaluate_max_cut(bitstring, graph_edges):
    cut_value = 0
    # Reverse string to match Qiskit's little-endian bit order
    rev_bit = bitstring[::-1]
    for u, v in graph_edges:
        if rev_bit[u] != rev_bit[v]:
            cut_value += 1
    return cut_value

for bitstring in counts.keys():
    c_val = evaluate_max_cut(bitstring, edges)
    print(f"Bitstring |{bitstring}\\rangle -> Cut Size: {c_val} (Frequency: {counts[bitstring]})")

7. Caveats & Real-World Limits

  • Barren Plateaus & Local Minima: Like VQE, high-dimensional parameter landscapes for large \(p\) or large qubit counts suffer from vanishing gradients and non-convex energy landscapes, causing classical optimisers to get trapped in suboptimal local minima.
  • Hardware Connectivity Limits: Real hardware topology rarely matches arbitrary graph structures. Mapping non-adjacent graph edges requires inserting additional \(SWAP\) gates, significantly increasing circuit depth and gate noise.
  • Classical Benchmark Comparison: Highly efficient classical approximation techniques (such as the Goemans-Williamson algorithm, which guarantees an approximation ratio of \(\approx 0.878\) for Max-Cut in polynomial time) set a high performance baseline that near-term NISQ hardware struggles to surpass.

8. Applications

  • Network Partitioning & Portfolio Optimisation: Solving Max-Cut and graph clustering problems in telecommunications routing and financial asset diversification.
  • Logistics & Scheduling: Mapping constraints in vehicle routing problems (VRP) and job-shop scheduling to Ising Hamiltonians.
  • Combinatorial Satisfiability: Solving constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) and 3-SAT instances across quantum-assisted scheduling domains.
