Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)

Flagship & Hybrid Models

1. Overview & Problem Definition

The Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) is a flagship hybrid quantum-classical algorithm designed to find the ground state energy (the lowest eigenvalue) of a given physical system, which is mathematically represented by a matrix called a Hamiltonian (\(H\)).

Simulating quantum chemistry and molecular dynamics is one of the most promising applications of quantum computing. While Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE) can theoretically find these energies with perfect accuracy, its circuit depth is far too long for modern, noisy quantum hardware. VQE solves this problem by offloading the heavy mathematical optimisation to a classical computer, keeping the quantum circuit extremely short and noise-resilient.

Classical Complexity

\(\mathcal{O}(2^N)\)

Finding the eigenvalues of an exponentially large \(2^N \times 2^N\) matrix scales terribly for classical computers.

Quantum Speedup

Heuristic

VQE does not offer a strict, provable mathematical speedup like Shor's algorithm. It is a heuristic algorithm that provides a practical, polynomial-time approximation for near-term hardware.

2. Intuition

VQE relies on a fundamental rule of quantum mechanics called the Variational Principle. This principle states that if you guess any valid quantum state (a wave function) and measure its energy, the result will always be greater than or equal to the true ground state energy of the system. You can never guess an energy lower than the absolute minimum.

VQE turns this principle into a game of "hot and cold":

  1. The Quantum Guess: The quantum computer prepares a parametrised guess state (an ansatz) using gates with adjustable knobs (angles). It then measures the energy of this state.
  2. The Classical Update: The classical computer looks at the measured energy and uses an optimisation algorithm (like gradient descent) to adjust the knobs on the quantum gates, trying to find a configuration that yields a slightly lower energy.
  3. The Loop: This loop repeats thousands of times. The classical computer continuously tunes the quantum circuit until the energy stops dropping. At this point, the system has converged on the best possible approximation of the ground state.

3. Required Gates & Circuit Schematic

  • Parametrised Rotation Gates (\(RX(\theta), RY(\theta), RZ(\theta)\)): These act as the adjustable "knobs" the classical optimizer controls.
  • Entangling Gates (\(CX\) or \(CZ\)): Used to create quantum correlations between qubits, allowing the ansatz to represent complex molecular bonds.
  • Measurement in Pauli Bases: To evaluate the Hamiltonian, the qubits must be measured across the \(X\), \(Y\), and \(Z\) axes.

4. Mathematical Proof & State Evolution

Step 1: The Variational Principle

VQE computes the ground state energy $E_0$ of a quantum system defined by a Hamiltonian $H$. It relies on the Variational Principle, which states that the expectation value of the energy for any trial wavefunction $|\psi\rangle$ is bounded below by the true ground state energy:

\[ \langle H \rangle = \langle \psi | H | \psi \rangle \ge E_0 \]

By framing this as an optimization problem, we can find the ground state by varying the parameters of the trial state $|\psi\rangle$ to minimize the expectation value $\langle H \rangle$.

Step 2: Hamiltonian Decomposition & Ansatz Preparation

To execute on quantum hardware, the physical Hamiltonian is mapped into a weighted sum of tensor products of Pauli matrices $P_i \in \{I, X, Y, Z\}^{\otimes n}$:

\[ H = \sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i P_i \]

The quantum computer prepares a parameterized trial state, known as the ansatz, using a variational circuit $U(\vec{\theta})$ applied to the ground state:

\[ |\psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle = U(\vec{\theta})|0\rangle^{\otimes n} \]

where $\vec{\theta}$ represents the classical parameters (e.g., rotation angles).

Step 3: Expectation Value Evaluation

The total energy $E(\vec{\theta})$ of the trial state is measured. By exploiting the linearity of expectation values, the quantum computer measures the individual Pauli strings, and the total energy is reconstructed classically:

\[ E(\vec{\theta}) = \langle \psi(\vec{\theta}) | H | \psi(\vec{\theta}) \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i \langle \psi(\vec{\theta}) | P_i | \psi(\vec{\theta}) \rangle \]

Because quantum measurements are probabilistic, each term $\langle P_i \rangle$ is estimated by running the circuit multiple times (shots) and averaging the observed $+1$ and $-1$ eigenvalues.

Step 4: Classical Optimization & Convergence

Once the total energy $E(\vec{\theta})$ is calculated, a classical optimizer updates the parameters $\vec{\theta}$ to navigate the energy landscape downwards (often using gradient descent and the quantum parameter-shift rule):

\[ \vec{\theta}_{new} = \text{Optimizer}(E(\vec{\theta}), \vec{\theta}) \]

This hybrid loop—quantum state preparation, measurement, and classical parameter updating—repeats iteratively. The system descends through Hilbert space until the energy converges to a minimum.

Conclusion: At convergence, the optimized parameter vector defines a state $|\psi(\vec{\theta}_{opt})\rangle$ that closely approximates the true ground state, and the resulting expectation value provides an upper-bound estimate of the ground state energy $E_0$.

5. Interactive Visualisation

  • Energy Convergence Graph: A live 2D line graph tracking the energy \(E(\vec{\theta})\) over each iteration step. The user can watch the curve drop sharply and then slowly level out as it asymptotes toward a dashed line representing the true, classical simulated ground state.
  • Parameter Optimization Landscape: A 3D topographical map representing the cost function surface. A marker representing the current parameters \(\vec{\theta}\) visually "rolls" down the hills into the lowest valley as the classical optimiser iterates.

import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit.circuit.library import EfficientSU2
from qiskit.quantum_info import SparsePauliOp, Statevector, DensityMatrix, Operator
from qiskit_algorithms.optimizers import COBYLA
from qiskit.primitives import StatevectorEstimator
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit.visualization import plot_histogram, plot_bloch_multivector, plot_state_qsphere, array_to_latex
from IPython.display import display, Markdown

# ==========================================
# CONFIGURATION & PROBLEM DEFINITION
# ==========================================
# 1. Define the Hamiltonian (e.g., a simple molecule or spin model)
hamiltonian = SparsePauliOp.from_list([("ZZ", 1.0), ("XX", -0.5), ("IY", 0.2)])
num_qubits = hamiltonian.num_qubits

# 2. Define the Ansatz (Hardware-efficient heuristic)
ansatz = EfficientSU2(num_qubits=num_qubits, reps=1, entanglement='linear')

# ==========================================
# CLASSICAL OPTIMISATION LOOP
# ==========================================
estimator = StatevectorEstimator()
optimiser = COBYLA(maxiter=100)
energy_history = []

def cost_function(params):
    """Evaluates the expectation value <ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)>"""

    pub = (ansatz, hamiltonian, [params])
    job = estimator.run([pub])

    energy = job.result()[0].data.evs[0]

    energy_history.append(energy)
    return energy

# Random initialisation for parameters
np.random.seed(42) # Seeded for reproducible lab results
initial_theta = np.random.rand(ansatz.num_parameters)

# 3. Run Optimisation
print("Running VQE Optimisation...")
result = optimiser.minimize(fun=cost_function, x0=initial_theta)
optimal_energy = result.fun
optimal_params = result.x

# ==========================================
# EXTRACT EXACT MATH OF THE OPTIMAL STATE
# ==========================================
# Bind the classical optimiser's results to the quantum circuit
optimal_circuit = ansatz.assign_parameters(optimal_params)
optimal_circuit.barrier(label="Optimum State")

unitary = Operator(optimal_circuit)
final_state = Statevector(optimal_circuit)
final_density = DensityMatrix(optimal_circuit)

# Create a copy for Z-basis measurement
meas_circuit = optimal_circuit.copy()
meas_circuit.measure_all()

# Execute on AerSimulator for histogram
simulator = AerSimulator()
tqc = transpile(meas_circuit, simulator)
job = simulator.run(tqc, shots=1024)
counts = job.result().get_counts()

# ==========================================
# REQUIRED COURSEWORK OUTPUTS
# ==========================================

display(Markdown(f"VQE Analysis: Ground State Estimation"))
print(f"Optimal Energy Found: {optimal_energy:.6f}")
print(f"Total Iterations: {len(energy_history)}")

display(Markdown("1. Circuit Visualisation (Bound with Optimal Parameters)"))
display(optimal_circuit.draw('mpl'))

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.circuit.library import EfficientSU2
from qiskit.quantum_info import SparsePauliOp
from qiskit_algorithms.optimizers import COBYLA
from qiskit_primitives import Estimator
import numpy as np

def run_qiskit_vqe():
    # 1. Define the Hamiltonian (e.g., a simple molecule mapping)
    hamiltonian = SparsePauliOp.from_list([("ZZ", 1.0), ("XX", -0.5), ("IY", 0.2)])
    
    # 2. Define the Ansatz (Hardware-efficient heuristic)
    ansatz = EfficientSU2(num_qubits=2, reps=1, entanglement='linear')
    
    # 3. Define the Estimator primitive and Optimizer
    estimator = Estimator()
    optimizer = COBYLA(maxiter=100)
    
    # 4. Objective function
    def cost_function(params):
        # The estimator directly calculates <psi(params)| H |psi(params)>
        job = estimator.run(ansatz, hamiltonian, parameter_values=params)
        result = job.result().values[0]
        return result
        
    # 5. Run Optimization
    initial_theta = np.random.rand(ansatz.num_parameters)
    result = optimizer.minimize(fun=cost_function, x0=initial_theta)
    
    return result.fun, result.x

optimal_energy, optimal_params = run_qiskit_vqe()
print(f"Optimal Energy Found: {optimal_energy:.4f}")

7. Caveats & Real-World Limits

  • Barren Plateaus: For large, deeply parametrised circuits, the optimisation landscape becomes completely flat. The gradients (the slope pointing the optimiser towards the minimum) vanish exponentially as the number of qubits grows, making it impossible for the classical computer to know which way to adjust the knobs.
  • Measurement Overhead: A realistic molecular Hamiltonian can decompose into millions of individual Pauli strings. Because VQE requires executing the circuit thousands of times per Pauli string at every single iteration step, the total hardware runtime can quickly become physically prohibitive.
  • Local Minima: Because VQE relies on classical heuristic optimisers, it is prone to getting stuck in local valleys in the energy landscape, returning a slightly incorrect ground state energy.

8. Applications

  • Quantum Chemistry: Used to simulate molecular structures (like \(H_2\), \(LiH\), or \(BeH_2\)) to find their ground state binding energies, which is critical for drug discovery and material design.
  • Combinatorial Optimisation: A variant of VQE known as the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) maps logistical problems (like the Travelling Salesperson or Max-Cut) to a Hamiltonian and uses the exact same variational loop to find the optimal classical solution.
  • Condensed Matter Physics: Simulating strongly correlated electron systems and lattice models (like the Fermi-Hubbard model) to study high-temperature superconductivity.
